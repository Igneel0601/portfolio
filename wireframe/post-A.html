Entwined Lives

Exploring the intricate relationships between plants and animals, challenging the traditional categorization of flora and fauna.

essay · ecology · 8 min read · 1,847 words · 2026·05·20 reading-the-living-world.md

The forest teeming with life, where trees stretch towards the sky and roots delve deep into the earth, it's easy to assume that plants and animals exist in separate realms. But this dichotomy is more than just a convenient way to organize our understanding of nature — it's a fundamental mistake. The line between flora and fauna blurs when we look closely at the intricate web of relationships that binds these organisms together.

My correlated worlds

One place to start this journey is with mycorrhizal networks, the hidden fungal connections that crisscross the forest floor. These underground pathways allow plants to share nutrients and information with each other, creating a vast, unseen network that defies our traditional notions of individuality. A single tree's roots can span acres, linking it to scores of neighbors in a symbiotic relationship that is at once mutualistic and cooperative.

But mycorrhizal networks aren't just limited to plants. They also connect with other organisms — like fungi and even animals, which can tap into this underground resource. The fungus Pisolithus arrhizus forms relationships with both trees and insects, demonstrating that the boundaries between plant and animal are far more permeable than we often assume.

The permeable boundary

Once you start to see these connections, you can't unsee them. The orchid that mimics a wasp; the ant that farms the aphid; the fungus that turns the carpenter ant into a marionette. We name them, we file them in kingdoms, and then they slip the file and walk into another room. Taxonomy is a useful fiction — until it isn't.

The deeper you look, the less the categories hold. Plants behave; animals photosynthesize (the sea slug Elysia chlorotica steals chloroplasts from algae and runs them for months). The categories are for us, not for the world.
